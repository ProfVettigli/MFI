Time Travel

From H. G. Wells to Hawking: explore the physics, philosophy, and narrative structures behind one of humanity's oldest scientific fantasies.

What Is Time Travel?

Time travel — moving between different points in time as freely as we move through space — has fascinated scientists and storytellers alike for centuries. What began as pure fantasy gradually became a legitimate area of scientific inquiry after Einstein's theory of relativity revealed that time is not the fixed, universal backdrop Newton imagined.

Modern physics does not rule out all forms of time travel. In fact, forward time travel is not only possible — it happens every day, at scales too small to notice. The deeper question is whether backward time travel is physically realizable, and what it would mean for causality, free will, and the structure of reality itself.

In this lesson we will explore three frameworks for thinking about time travel: the Fixed Past model, the Branching Timelines model, and the Block Universe model. Each has roots in both physics and narrative fiction, and each gives a radically different answer to the paradoxes time travel creates.

A Brief History of Time Travel

1895

H. G. Wells publishes The Time Machine — the first major work of fiction to treat time travel as a technological achievement rather than magic. Wells introduced the idea of time as a "fourth dimension" navigable by machine, decades before Einstein formalized the concept.

1905

Albert Einstein publishes his Special Theory of Relativity. The central consequence for time: time dilation. A clock moving at high velocity ticks more slowly relative to a stationary observer. Forward time travel is therefore not science fiction — it is a measurable physical effect.

1915

Einstein's General Theory of Relativity extends the framework: massive objects curve spacetime, and extreme gravitational fields also slow time. Near a black hole, time passes more slowly — the closer you are, the greater the effect.

1949

Kurt Gödel discovers an exact solution to Einstein's field equations that contains closed timelike curves (CTCs) — paths through spacetime that loop back on themselves, theoretically allowing a traveller to return to their own past.

1992

Stephen Hawking proposes the Chronology Protection Conjecture: the laws of physics conspire to prevent backward time travel at a macroscopic scale, preserving causality and preventing paradoxes. Hawking quipped: "The best evidence we have that time travel is not possible, and never will be, is that we have not been invaded by hordes of tourists from the future."

Three Models of Time

How does time travel actually work — if it works at all? Three competing models dominate both scientific and narrative discussions. Select each to explore its logic, its visual structure, and its most famous fictional example.

Fixed Past (Novikov Self-Consistency)

In this model, time is a single, unalterable timeline. If you travel to the past, you were always there — your actions are already part of history and cannot change the outcome. Paradoxes are logically impossible because the universe enforces self-consistency. This view, formalized by physicist Igor Novikov, implies that free will in time travel is an illusion: you can only do what you were always going to do.

Past             Present             Future   (one fixed line)
Classic example: 12 Monkeys (1995) — Bruce Willis cannot change the past; every attempt is already part of the original timeline.

Branching Timelines (Many-Worlds)

Every intervention in the past creates a new branch of reality, diverging from the original timeline. The traveller who goes back does not change their past — they create an alternate universe. This model, loosely inspired by Hugh Everett's Many-Worlds interpretation of quantum mechanics, resolves the grandfather paradox cleanly: killing your grandfather only affects the new branch, not the one you came from.

Original timeline
New branch (altered past)
Classic example: Back to the Future (1985) — Marty's actions create divergent timelines; the original 1985 still exists "elsewhere."

Block Universe (Eternalism)

In the Block Universe (or Eternalist) view, past, present, and future all exist simultaneously in a four-dimensional spacetime "block." Time is not something that flows — all moments coexist equally. This view is fully consistent with relativity. Time travel here means navigating to a different spacetime coordinate, much like moving through space. There is no privileged "now" — your present is just a slice of an eternal structure.

PAST — already exists
NOW — your current slice
FUTURE — also exists, equally real
All slices coexist in the 4D block
Classic example: Interstellar (2014) — Cooper navigates a tesseract where all moments of his daughter's life coexist simultaneously.

Time Dilation Calculator

Einstein's Special Relativity predicts that a traveller moving at a significant fraction of the speed of light ages more slowly than a stationary observer. This is time dilation, described by the Lorentz factor. If a traveller moves at velocity v for a period of time t (measured by the stationary observer), the traveller's own elapsed time τ is:

τ = t ⋅ √(1 − v²/c²)
